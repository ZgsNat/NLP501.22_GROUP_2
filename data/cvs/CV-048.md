Summary
Principal quality engineer with thirteen years in testing, the last six designing the way a 20-team engineering organisation tests and releases. I build shared testing foundations and decide, with engineering leaders, which checks must pass before code reaches customers.

Experience

Principal Quality Engineer, Subscription video platform
Jan 2020 – Present

I joined to fix a situation where every team tested differently and nobody trusted the release process. I started by reading the pipelines of all 20 teams and interviewing the engineers, then proposed a layered approach: fast checks near the code, service-to-service compatibility checks, a thin layer of full-journey checks, and a small number of production probes.

What I designed and run:
• A reusable testing foundation, packaged as versioned libraries and pipeline templates in two languages, used by 17 of 20 teams. Teams get the same reporting, the same data helpers and the same rules for retries and timeouts, but choose their own cases. I maintain the roadmap and a deprecation policy, and a small group of five engineers works on it with me.
• A scheme in which each service publishes the expectations that its consumers rely on, and the provider's pipeline verifies them before deploying. Over 60 services take part. In the first year this caught about 40 incompatible changes that would otherwise have been found in the shared staging environment or, worse, in production.
• Release gates in the delivery pipelines. A change can only go to customers if the critical journeys pass completely, the unreliable-test rate is below an agreed limit, load figures stay inside the limits we stored for each service, and no new high-severity findings come from the dependency scan. The gates are code in a shared template, so teams cannot quietly turn them off; exceptions are granted for 14 days at a time and listed publicly.
• The infrastructure that makes it affordable: an orchestrated cluster that starts isolated, short-lived environments per branch, and a pool of browser and device runners that scales with demand to roughly 300 parallel sessions. Median time from commit to verified result fell from 55 minutes to 12.
• A weekly quality report for engineering directors: escaped defects, rollbacks, time to restore, unreliable tests, and cost of the test infrastructure per team.
• Coaching: office hours for developers, a six-week programme for QA engineers moving from manual to code-based testing, and a yearly review of each team's testing approach with its tech lead.
